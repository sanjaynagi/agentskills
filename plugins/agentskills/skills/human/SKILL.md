# Human

Two problems, one fix. Bureaucratic prose is needlessly hard to read. AI slop reads like a machine wrote it. Both come from the same habit: reaching for the inflated version of a sentence instead of the plain one. So fix them in the same pass.

## Before rewriting, ask

Who reads this, and what do they need to do or know afterwards? Plain is relative to the reader. A passage a specialist finds plain can be opaque to everyone else. Settle that before touching a word, or state the assumption you're working from.

## What to change

- **"You," active voice, common words.** "You may apply," not "applicants may apply." "We sent it," not "it was sent." "Use," not "utilize." "Help," not "facilitate."
- **Verbs, not nominalisations.** "Decide" beats "make a decision."
- **UK spelling and usage, not US.** Organise, not organize. Colour, behaviour, favour. Centre, metre. Travelled, modelling, labelled. Analyse, not analyze. Licence for the noun, practise for the verb. Programme, except for software. Leave code, identifiers, filenames, and direct quotations exactly as they are.
- **One idea per sentence. Vary the length.** Short sentences for the load-bearing points. Don't let every sentence land at the same mid-length cadence. That evenness is itself a tell.
- **Front-load.** The point goes near the top of the paragraph or section, not the end.
- **Concrete over abstract.** "Write the date on the form," not "indicate the temporal coordinates."
- **Cut, don't pad.** If a phrase can go without losing meaning, it goes.

## Cut on sight

- **Em/en dashes (—, –) and `--`.** Replace with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses. Zero tolerance: scan the final draft for `—` and `–` before you deliver it. A spaced hyphen used the same way is the same tell.
- **AI vocabulary:** delve, crucial, intricate, tapestry, landscape (abstract), testament, underscore(s), pivotal, garner, foster, showcase, boast(s), vibrant, nestled, in the heart of.
- **Significance-inflation:** "stands as a testament to," "marks a pivotal moment," "underscores its importance," "reflects broader trends." Say the plain fact instead.
- **Vague authorities:** "experts believe," "industry observers note" with no named source. Cite the source or cut the claim.
- **Superficial -ing tails:** "...highlighting its role," "...fostering collaboration" bolted onto a sentence for fake depth. Cut the tail.
- **Rule-of-three padding and elegant variation.** Don't force triads. Don't cycle synonyms for the same referent (protagonist, hero, main character, central figure: pick one).
- **Copula avoidance:** "serves as," "stands as," "boasts" where plain "is" or "has" would do.
- **Negative parallelisms and tailing negations:** "not just X, it's Y"; "...no guessing" tacked on. Write the real clause.
- **Filler and hedging:** "in order to," "due to the fact that," "it is important to note that," "could potentially possibly." Cut to the claim.
- **Weasel words and doublets:** "where appropriate," "any and all," "null and void." Pick one word, or name the actual contingency.
- **Formatting tells:** title-case headings, emoji bullets, bold-header vertical lists ("**Security:** Security has been..."), curly quotes in plain text, throat-clearing right after a heading ("Speed matters." then the real point).
- **Chatbot residue:** "I hope this helps!", "Let's dive in," "Great question!" Delete it. It isn't content.
- **Generic upbeat closers:** "the future looks bright," "exciting times ahead." Either state the concrete next fact or end the piece.
- **Formulaic "Challenges and Future" sections,** and knowledge-cutoff or speculative filler ("not publicly available, suggesting she keeps a low profile"). State what's known, or cut the section.

## Don't over-correct

Not every instance of these patterns is AI-written. Perfect grammar, formal vocabulary used precisely, a single "however," curly quotes from an auto-formatter, one em dash from a human editor. None of these alone is evidence. Look for **clusters**. And preserve real human signal when you find it: specific hard-to-fabricate detail, mixed feelings left unresolved, genuine asides, uneven sentence rhythm. Don't flatten those chasing "clean."

How hard you push depends on the kind of writing. Technical, legal, and reference writing should stay neutral and plain, so don't inject opinion or a first-person voice there. In an essay, a blog post, or personal writing, a flat, opinion-free, evenly paced draft is itself a tell: add a real reaction, let a tangent through, vary the rhythm.

## Running a pass

1. Establish the audience and task, or state your assumption.
2. Draft the rewrite: apply the moves above, delete anything on the cut-on-sight list.
3. Re-scan the draft once for what's *still* obviously AI or bureaucratic. Fix those specifically.
4. Deliver it. Name the three to five biggest changes you made. Skip the line-by-line log.

Don't drop coverage. The rewrite should still say everything the original said. Expect it to run shorter anyway, because most of what you cut was padding.
